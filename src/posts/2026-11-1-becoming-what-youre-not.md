# Becoming What You're Not

While looking back through my previous high school work, I stumbled across this piece which I had written during 10th grade. This piece is something that I'm quite proud of, since it was the first piece where I felt I actually opened up. It made me less of a closed shell. 

The explorer, Ferdinand Magellan, circumnavigated the world on a wooden ship regardless of feasibility, and I attempted to learn calculus in 6th grade. Coming into middle school, I wasn’t anything special. I never really excelled at anything, no purpose or meaning. I kind of just *existed*, and this was further propelled by the stagnant feeling of the pandemic. I was intrigued by things I didn’t understand, knowing that it would make me look smart if I did. I tried to act as if I didn’t need validation, but back then, I did. This was me pretending to be something I’m not. I lacked a natural intuition; everything I knew came from what people told me, not what I discovered myself. I was no explorer. My lazy, couch potato personality turned into my greatest asset; it allowed me to persevere through what seemed like boring ZOOM classes, taking in information from one ear and releasing it from the other. The feeling of the unknown is curious to me, so I was naturally attracted to it. 
The cornerstone of my interest was found in 3Blue1Brown, a YouTuber who made elegant math explanations that I never understood. Something about the way he presented his content made it attractive to anyone, regardless of background, and it welcomed me. 
Most of the time during virtual learning, I lay back in my chair, with my camera off and notebook half-filled with doodles, struggling to remain focused while someone I didn’t know explained elementary concepts to me over a laggy interface. YouTube was more of an enrichment experience than a homework assignment. I watched because I wanted to learn more. I’d rewind the animation two or three times, still not understanding. But there wasn’t an audience; it was for myself, not for the expectations of others. That experience was new. 

Yet I still felt left out. 

Something was missing. 

I was interested in the content, but just like my experience in class, the information walked the plank as soon as it entered my noggin. I remained drawn to my screen, engrossed by the twisting sine curve and differential equations, all seeming like witchcraft. 
Something was still missing.

I’d like to say that I enjoyed feeling alone during the pandemic, but that would be a lie. As tough as I could act, it didn’t matter. With the curiosity that I had about confusion, I needed to share it with someone. The isolation didn’t make that possible. Returning to school was a grace that I didn’t deserve. After all, my interest in math was fraudulent, built on nothing more than a lie. I didn’t understand these things, I acted as if I did. However, it wasn’t all that bad, though. They say “curiosity killed the cat,” only it wasn’t killing me, it was making me stronger. I could piece together lessons quicker because of the foundation I created during the pandemic. Turns out it wasn’t as useless as I originally believed. Maybe the effort is what matters. 

By being in an environment where people give in to curiosity just as I do, I was able to find people who had similar interests to mine. We’re all curious in our ways; maybe not academically, but without some element of curiosity, what’s the point? Here and there, I witness people afraid to ask their questions in chemistry because of their fear of being wrong. We’ve built a world where we reward accuracy, and that inherently creates a fear of being incorrect. 

Something was missing.

I found it. 

Still interested in my surroundings, but less so than my clueless 6th-grade self. The explorer and couch potato features remain, but less so than my clueless 6th-grade self. My attraction towards the unknown is the same as my clueless 6th-grade self. Was my 6th-grade self so clueless? Clueless means having no understanding of something. That aspect is true, I really couldn’t do much, except just listen. But I was curious, wasn’t I? Suppose I’m cluelessly curious. I had wondered, and that’s what’s important. 

The 6th-grade 3 a.m. motivation was enough to set my plan in motion. Was this plan the best approach? No. Do I regret it? Never. It’s a plan that gives me intuition now. I sit in class, still cluelessly curious, filled with wonder about what I don’t know. Others are curious similarly, and they’re just waiting to be discovered. After all, there’s an endless sea of information waiting to be explored. Be the internet surfer. Surf the wave of expectation because sometimes we need to brute force our problems.  

Be the explorer that finds them, but also yourself in the process. Be Magellan. Approaching the seemingly impossible allowed me to belong to a group that would respect me and encourage me to stay curious… *cluelessly*.
